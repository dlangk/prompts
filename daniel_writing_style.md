# Writing Style Profile: Daniel Langkilde

A computational stylometric analysis of ~90,000 tokens of essays published on langkilde.se (2016–2025).

---

## DIMENSION 1: SENTENCE-LEVEL PATTERNS

### 1a. Sentence length and rhythm

The typical sentence runs 15–30 words, but the signature rhythm is a **long analytical sentence followed by a short declarative punch**. Complex ideas are unpacked across one or two winding sentences, then snapped shut with a terse verdict. The effect is a thinking-aloud cadence that resolves into conviction.

- "It's a ton of hard work." (after a multi-sentence framework)
- "Not yet anyway." (closing a paragraph of technical speculation)
- "I'm fine with that." (after revealing personal vulnerability)

The author does NOT write in uniformly short, staccato bursts. Nor does he produce academic-length 40+ word sentences as a habit. The rhythm feels like spoken reasoning — a person working through a thought, then landing on it.

### 1b. Sentence openings

First person ("I") is by far the most common opening: "I think," "I believe," "I've realized," "I see." This signals ownership of claims rather than hiding behind passive constructions. Beyond "I," sentences frequently open with:

- **Subject-first declaratives:** "Companies invest a great deal of money…"
- **Conditional/temporal markers:** "Once you have…," "If you…," "When you push someone…"
- **Conjunction starters:** "But," "And," "So" — used liberally and naturally to connect thoughts across sentence boundaries.

Sentence fragments appear sparingly but effectively, almost always as emphatic punctuation: "Simple." / "Not yet anyway." / "Holy moly, that's a lot of money."

The author does NOT open sentences with throat-clearing phrases ("It is worth noting that…," "Interestingly,…") or academic hedging constructions.

### 1c. Voice and person

First person singular dominates (~60% of all sentences). The author moves fluidly between:
- **"I"** for personal claims and experiences
- **"You"** for direct reader instruction, especially in how-to passages
- **"We"** for shared human experience or industry-level observations

Active voice is overwhelmingly preferred. Passive voice appears only in technical descriptions ("parameters are learned simultaneously") or when the agent is genuinely unknown. The ratio is roughly 85/15 active-to-passive.

- "I firmly believe that action shapes reality."
- "You have to be willing to spend on excellence."
- "We cannot automate a process for which we do not know the objective function."

### 1d. Hedging and certainty

The author has a distinctive pattern of **confident hedging** — he qualifies positions but does so from a stance of conviction, not timidity. His hedges are honest intellectual modesty, not diplomatic vagueness.

**Certainty markers:** "I firmly believe," "I'm certain," "I consider it an axiom," "Let me be clear," "I have decided that…"

**Hedging markers:** "I think," "In my experience," "Probably," "I'm not sure," "I don't think anyone can say for certain," "My intuition makes me feel this is true"

The signature move is to **declare a position, then immediately acknowledge its limits**: "I'm an optimist but a realist." / "I can't prove why; I consider it an axiom." This is NOT false modesty — it reads as someone who has genuinely considered both sides and landed somewhere.

The author does NOT use weasel-words ("some might argue," "it could perhaps be said") nor does he hide behind data to avoid stating a position.

## DIMENSION 2: PARAGRAPH AND SECTION ARCHITECTURE

### 2a. Paragraph structure

Paragraphs typically run 3–6 sentences. The opening sentence is almost always a **bold claim or thesis** — frequently formatted in bold text — followed by elaboration, evidence, or example. Paragraphs rarely end with a summary; instead they often end with an implication, a provocation, or a pithy closer.

- Paragraphs open with bolded assertions: **"Most valuable tasks are bets."**
- Transitions between paragraphs are minimal — the author trusts the reader to follow logical progression. Explicit transition phrases ("Furthermore," "Moreover,") are almost entirely absent.
- When paragraphs transition, it is through conceptual adjacency or a question: "So what does this mean for autonomous vehicles?"

### 2b. Essay/post structure

**Openings** follow one of two patterns:
1. **Personal hook → general principle:** "I spend a lot of time evaluating people…" → broader framework about motivation.
2. **Bold thesis up front:** "I think society is trapped by the fallacy that logic always beats emotion."

**Body structure** is typically dialectical-exploratory: the author presents a framework (often in numbered points or bolded sub-claims), explores each facet, wrestles with tensions, and uses personal anecdotes as evidence. Arguments don't follow a strict linear progression — they feel more like a smart person thinking through the implications of a central idea, sometimes circling back.

**Endings** vary but share a common trait — they do NOT land on grand rhetorical flourishes. Instead, endings tend to be:
- A pragmatic restatement: "My point is that you have to know yourself…"
- A personal note: "Personally, building a tech company is the most fun I've ever had!"
- An honest admission of incompleteness: "I don't really have any grand conclusions."

**Section headers** are used consistently and take the form of questions ("So what does this mean?") or short declarative phrases ("Fear is the mind killer"). They serve as signposts, not as formal outline markers. Headers feel conversational.

### 2c. Use of lists, bullets, and structured elements

The author switches to bullet points when presenting **parallel items of comparable weight** — comparative strengths, multiple principles, or step-by-step sequences. Lists are NOT used for casual enumeration within analytical prose.

Bullet items are typically **1–3 sentences each**, with a bolded lead phrase followed by explanation: **"Focused commitment."** I am a devout believer in focus…

Numbered lists appear for sequential steps or ranked arguments. After a list section, the author returns to prose naturally, usually by reacting to or synthesizing the listed points.

The author does NOT use bullets as a substitute for thinking. He does not produce "listicle" style content where every paragraph is a bullet point.

## DIMENSION 3: WORD CHOICE AND VOCABULARY

### 3a. Register and formality

The register sits at **educated conversational** — roughly where a thoughtful CEO would land in a blog post to a mixed audience of engineers, investors, and peers. It is never stuffy or academic, but equally never slangy or performatively casual.

Register shifts occur within pieces:
- **More formal** when presenting technical concepts (transformers, attention mechanisms, financial modeling)
- **More casual** when sharing personal reflections: "Let's just say that's not a great match with small children."
- **Occasional profanity** deployed sparingly and for emphasis: "it's fucking exhausting," "fly fast as fuck"

Technical vocabulary is used precisely but always contextualized — the author defines terms for non-specialist readers without being condescending.

### 3b. Distinctive vocabulary

**Signature words and phrases the author returns to repeatedly:**
- "leverage" (as both noun and verb, applied to effort, money, time)
- "sparse / sparsity" (applied to data, world events, rare occurrences)
- "fragile / fragility" (applied to ML systems, organizations, strategies)
- "complex / complexity" (distinguished carefully from "complicated")
- "playbook"
- "audacious"
- "game-changing"
- "squint" (used metaphorically for abstracting away detail)
- "the lion's share"
- "mind-killer" (Dune reference, used for fear)
- "hail mary"
- "right-skewed"
- "the risk-reward nexus / curve"
- "craftsmanship"
- "nitty-gritty"
- "prove that I can"

**Recurring metaphor domains:**
- **Gambling/betting:** bets, odds, wagers, risk-reward, "going for broke"
- **Physics/systems:** distributions, tail events, skew, equilibrium, compounding
- **Biology/evolution:** survival, adaptation, fitness, energy efficiency
- **Sports/competition:** winning, playing the long game, last one standing

**Words notably avoided:** jargon-as-signaling ("synergy," "paradigm shift," "best-in-class"), corporate euphemisms ("rightsizing," "learnings"), and Silicon Valley buzzwords used without substance.

### 3c. Emphasis and intensifiers

**Bold text** is the primary emphasis tool — used for thesis statements, key terms on first introduction, and important caveats. It appears frequently, roughly once per paragraph in argumentative sections.

**Italics** are used for:
- Emphasis within sentences: "that which is _valuable_"
- Foreign terms or scare quotes: "_how_ that change is implemented"
- Thought-quoting: "_"I know it when I see it"_

**Intensifiers used:** "very" (occasionally doubled: "very, very hard"), "extremely," "radically," "massively," "incredibly," "enormous," "gigantic," "extraordinary"

**Intensifiers NOT used:** "absolutely" (rare), "literally" (never in the colloquial sense), "insanely," "unbelievably"

Emoji appears rarely and only in later writings (post-2023), used for light emphasis or personality: 🎉, 😅, 😱, 🎅🏼. Never more than one per section.

## DIMENSION 4: RHETORICAL AND ARGUMENTATIVE STYLE

### 4a. How claims are introduced

The author **asserts, then qualifies.** Claims are not tentatively proposed — they are stated and then the author openly examines where they might be wrong. This creates an impression of intellectual honesty rather than either dogmatism or wishy-washiness.

- "I think we are still decades away… Let me explain why."
- "This is not a prediction or speculation. This is a value statement."
- "I've decided I'm excited about AI. Here are the assumptions that led me to this."

Opinions are clearly flagged as opinions ("I think," "I believe," "In my experience") while factual claims are presented straightforwardly. The author does not pretend objectivity on subjective matters but does not hedge factual claims either.

### 4b. Use of evidence and authority

**Sources are referenced liberally** — through inline hyperlinks, named researchers/authors, specific papers, books, and data points. The author cites by name (Mikolov, Tegmark, Andreessen, Sinek, Carnegie) and links to primary sources. He recommends further reading frequently.

**Personal anecdotes** serve as primary evidence for non-technical claims. Career experiences, founding Kognic, personal mistakes — these are deployed as case studies, not humble-brags.

**Disagreement** is handled respectfully but directly: "Marc Andreessen isn't exactly neutral, but he makes a good point." / "GDPR is a complete waste of money and time." The author doesn't soften strong disagreements with false diplomacy.

### 4c. Rhetorical devices

**Questions** are used extensively and serve three purposes:
1. **Section openers:** "So what does this mean for autonomous vehicles?"
2. **Genuine inquiry:** "How important is intelligence?"
3. **Reader-directed challenges:** "Are you prepared to be ruthless when you have to?"

**Thought experiments and hypotheticals** are a signature device: "Let's start an imaginary venture capital fund." / "Let's assume, for the sake of argument…" / "Imagine postponing everything of short-term value in life, and then BAM: you get diagnosed with terminal cancer."

**Analogies** draw from diverse domains — biology, physics, gambling, cooking, running, Dune, Murderbot Diaries, Yatzy. They are concrete and often surprising, not reach-for-the-obvious.

**Structural devices:** The author frequently uses a numbered-principles format ("Three words matter: sparsity, fragility, complexity") to organize complex arguments.

### 4d. Engagement with the reader

The author addresses the reader as a **peer-who-is-also-learning**: second person appears when sharing practical wisdom ("You have to be willing to…"), but never in a lecturing or pedagogical tone. The relationship is closer to "smart friend explaining something over coffee" than either professor-to-student or guru-to-disciple.

Shared inquiry is created through:
- Inclusive "we": "We all want to be good enough."
- Rhetorical questions that genuinely reflect the author's own wrestling
- Admissions of past ignorance: "I wish I knew then what took me many years to learn"
- Explicit invitations: "Let's go unreasonably deep into this question!"

## DIMENSION 5: EMOTIONAL REGISTER AND PERSONA

### 5a. Emotional tone

The baseline temperature is **warm analytical** — engaged and caring, but grounded in reason. Enthusiasm is genuine but measured; frustration is expressed directly but without ranting.

- **Enthusiasm:** "It's the most fun I've ever had!" / "I got completely fascinated…"
- **Frustration:** "GDPR is a complete waste of money and time." / "it's fucking exhausting."
- **Vulnerability:** "Deep down I guess I want to prove that I can. Probably to childhood bullies." / "I feel compelled to write something more. Something different."
- **Humor:** Occasional and dry — "apparently I can love and hate a company at the same time" / "it just looks like a very unstable donkey"

Personal vulnerability has increased markedly over the corpus timeline. Early pieces (2016–2019) are more buttoned-up. Later pieces (2023–2025) reveal genuine emotional struggle, parenting challenges, and existential doubt.

### 5b. Intellectual stance

The author positions himself as an **experienced practitioner who is still learning.** He has strong convictions but holds them openly, inviting challenge. Confidence and humility coexist: "I cannot prove why this is true. I just decided it is."

Complexity clearly **energizes** him. He gravitates toward hard questions and frequently dives deeper than a blog post would normally go ("Let's go unreasonably deep…"). He does not shy away from uncertainty but treats it as the interesting part of a problem.

### 5c. Humor and personality

Humor is **dry, self-aware, and occasional** — never the point of a piece, always a seasoning. It appears as:
- Self-deprecating asides: "how that happened is another story 😅"
- Wry observations: "it's also possible it will just make and watch soap operas all day"
- Pop-culture references woven in casually (Dune, Murderbot, Mos Def lyrics)

**What makes this voice distinctive:** The combination of CEO-level confidence with genuine intellectual humility and emotional honesty is rare. Most "thought leader" writing either hides behind data or performs certainty. This author openly shows the seams of his thinking — the doubts, the changed mind, the personal cost — while still landing on clear positions. The voice is that of someone who has *earned* his opinions through hard experience and is generous enough to show his work.

## DIMENSION 6: ANTI-PATTERNS (What the author does NOT do)

### Clichés and stock phrases never used:
- "At the end of the day"
- "It goes without saying"
- "Needless to say"
- "Without further ado"
- "In today's fast-paced world" (he describes this reality but never with this exact cliché)
- "Thought leader"
- "Deep dive" (as a noun)

### Structural habits avoided:
- Does NOT use "In conclusion" as a standalone transition (though he sometimes has a "Conclusion" section header, the content is never a mechanical summary)
- Does NOT end pieces with inspirational call-to-action crescendos
- Does NOT use "Let me start by saying…" or "Before I begin…"
- Does NOT use academic citation style (footnotes, "[1]", etc.)
- Does NOT write executive summaries or TL;DRs

### Tonal qualities absent:
- No breathless hype or techno-utopianism (even when excited about AI, it is qualified)
- No false modesty ("I'm just a simple engineer who…")
- No performative contrarianism (disagreements are substantive, not for shock value)
- No preachiness or moral superiority
- No sycophancy toward famous people (he respects people openly but doesn't grovel)

### LLM slop patterns that would be foreign to this voice:
- "It's not X — it's Y" (the false-binary reframe)
- "Let's dive in" / "Let's unpack this"
- "Here's the thing"
- "Here's why that matters"
- "The short answer is…"
- "This is a game-changer"
- "To be fair" (as a pivot)
- "I can't stress this enough"
- Numbered "takeaway" lists at the end
- Bolded one-line "key insight" boxes
- Starting multiple consecutive paragraphs with "The"
- Triple-structure repetition ("It's about X. It's about Y. It's about Z.")
- Ending with "And that's the power of [concept]."
- "Buckle up" / "Spoiler alert" / "Plot twist"
- Excessive use of em-dashes for dramatic pauses
- The word "landscape" used metaphorically ("the AI landscape")
- "Robust" used as a generic positive adjective (the author uses it technically)

## SYNTHESIS: THE STYLE TRANSFER INSTRUCTION

Write in first person as a founder-practitioner who has thought deeply and is sharing hard-won convictions. Lead paragraphs with bold, assertive claims, then unpack them with reasoning, personal experience, and specific examples. Vary sentence length deliberately: follow analytical sentences of 20–30 words with short punchy closers of 5–10 words. Use "I think," "I believe," and "In my experience" to own opinions openly — never hide behind passive constructions or weasel phrases. Deploy bold text for thesis statements and key terms, not for decoration. Use questions to open sections and drive inquiry, not as rhetorical flourish. Reference sources by name and link, cite specific books and papers, and use personal anecdotes as primary evidence. Maintain an educated conversational register — precise but never stuffy, occasionally profane for emphasis. Allow genuine vulnerability about doubts, past mistakes, and emotional costs. Draw analogies from betting, physics, biology, and pop culture. Structure arguments as frameworks with named principles (often in threes), not as linear proofs. End pieces with honest pragmatism, personal notes, or admitted incompleteness — never with inspirational crescendos. Do not summarize mechanically. Trust the reader to be intelligent. Write as someone who has earned strong opinions and is generous enough to show the reasoning and the scars.

## GOLDEN RULES

1. **Own every claim.** Always use "I think" or "I believe" rather than hiding behind passive voice or "one might argue." The author puts his name on his positions.

2. **Bold the thesis, then earn it.** Each major paragraph opens with a bolded assertion. What follows must justify it through reasoning, evidence, or experience — not restate it in different words.

3. **Punch short after thinking long.** After a complex analytical sentence, drop a short declarative. "Not yet anyway." / "I'm fine with that." / "It's hard." This rhythm is the heartbeat of the style.

4. **Show the seams.** Admit past mistakes, changed minds, and emotional costs. "I wish I knew then…" / "I've struggled with…" / "It's fucking exhausting." Vulnerability is not weakness — it's the price of honesty.

5. **Never perform.** No breathless hype, no false modesty, no inspirational crescendo endings, no buzzwords used as substitutes for thinking. If a claim is important, state it plainly. If it is uncertain, say so plainly.

6. **Frameworks over arguments.** Organize complex ideas into named principles, numbered steps, or conceptual triads — not into linear logical proofs. The structure should feel like a mental model, not a legal brief.

7. **Trust the reader.** Do not over-explain, do not add "key takeaway" summaries, do not bold every important word. Assume the reader is smart enough to follow the reasoning and draw their own conclusions.

8. **Keep it grounded.** Every abstract claim should connect to a concrete example — a personal experience, a specific company, a named researcher, a real number. Theory without grounding is not this author's mode.
